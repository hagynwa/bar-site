# Hooks, retention and persuading parents: what the evidence says

Research for the "עוד קצת ילדות" series (8 vertical videos, 25-40 s, Hebrew, Reels / TikTok / Facebook / WhatsApp; notebook paper-collage style; local narrator; burned-in captions; ends in a pact to delay a smartphone until the end of 5th grade).

Compiled: November 2026. Every source below was looked up during this research. Where I could only reach a secondary summary, or could not confirm a number, I say so. Nothing unverified is used in the rules.

**Strength labels**

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **MA** | Meta-analysis of many studies |
| **EXP** | Randomised experiment (lab, online or field) |
| **QE** | Quasi-experiment (matched comparison, no randomisation) |
| **PD** | Platform data: Meta, TikTok or Google analysing its own ads or users. Usually correlational ("top ads tend to do X"), self-published and not peer-reviewed |
| **SURVEY** | Opinion poll (what people say, not what they do) |
| **EO** | Expert opinion, theory or platform advice with no published data behind it |
| **AN** | Anecdote or single case |

## (a) Summary: 15 rules for our 25-40 s videos

| # | Rule | Main evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Deliver the fact by second 3.** The first line is the finding itself in plain words, such as "A phone in the bedroom costs kids sleep." Do not save it for a reveal at the end. | TikTok: over 63% of its top-CTR videos show the key message in the first 3 s. Meta/Toluna: brand and main message in the first 5 s → 1.7x more likely to reach the top 20%. Google 2015: early elements raise recall. Scacco & Muddiman 2019: plain "summary" headlines beat curiosity-gap headlines in a lab and a field test. | PD (correlational) + EXP |
| 2 | **Hook with the parent's own child, and answer at once.** A question or "you" line aimed at the viewer works, but only if the answer arrives within about 2-4 s. Do not tease: a curiosity gap that is not closed reads as clickbait and costs credibility. | Lai & Farbrot 2014: self-referencing question headlines got about 175% more clicks than statements (field test). Question headlines were also rated *less* credible and expected to be less engaging (Engaging News Project; NSF-funded headline study). Loewenstein 1994: curiosity is short-lived and disappoints if the payoff is weak. | EXP (mixed) + theory |
| 3 | **Make the first frame work with the sound off, and the full video work with the sound on.** Put the hook as large burned-in text on frame 1, with something moving, and caption every spoken line. Still treat the voice as essential: Reels and TikTok are mostly watched with sound on. | Facebook (2016): captions raised view time by 12% on average, and 41% of video ads were "basically meaningless without sound". Verizon/Publicis 2019: 69% watch with sound off in public. TikTok/Kantar 2021: 88% of TikTok users say sound is essential. | PD + SURVEY |
| 4 | **Say it and show it at the same time.** The narration and the on-screen text and image carry the same message together, with music under the voice. | Meta/Toluna (2024 data, published 2025): speech plus music → 2.0x top-20% brand interest; message carried by both audio and visual cues → 1.8x. | PD |
| 5 | **One fact per video, about 25-35 s, built to be watched to the end and forwarded.** Cut anything that does not serve that fact. | TikTok guidance: "21-34 seconds performs best". Instagram (Mosseri 2023): the most important Reels predictions are whether you will reshare it and whether you will watch it all the way through. | PD / platform guidance |
| 6 | **Show a person early: a face, a child or a parent in an everyday moment.** In the notebook style this can be a drawn child's face or real hands in frame. The pact video should end on real local faces. | Google 2015: smiling characters and recognisable faces in the first 5 s were associated with more viewing and brand lift. Meta/Toluna: everyday "slice of life" scenes with people → 1.5x. TikTok: 33% of its highest-VTR ads speak straight to camera. | PD (correlational) |
| 7 | **Change something on screen every 2-4 s, and tie every change to the narration.** A new paper layer, a crayon stroke or a zoom works. Avoid random "pattern interrupts" that have nothing to do with the content. | Limited-capacity model (Lang 2000): cuts and edits trigger automatic orienting responses that grab attention, but cuts unrelated to the content are poorly remembered, especially when they come fast. | EXP (lab) + theory |
| 8 | **Keep the campaign mark light and pulsed.** No logo intro. Use a small consistent mark throughout and a short end card. | Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2010: brief repeated brand appearances reduce zapping, compared with one long appearance. Google 2015: brand in the first 5 s → more recall but also more skipping. TikTok: "Skip long intros or logo animations". Meta/Toluna: brand shown more than once → 1.8x. | EXP + PD |
| 9 | **Pair every harm with one easy, concrete action in the same video.** For example: "Tonight, phones charge in the kitchen." One-time actions work best, and signing the pact is one. | Tannenbaum et al. 2015: fear appeals work better with efficacy statements (95% CI d 0.31-0.55 vs 0.13-0.29 without) and for one-time behaviours (0.30-0.56 vs 0.14-0.29). Witte & Allen 2000: strong fear with low efficacy produced the most defensive responses. Peters et al. 2013: threat worked only under high efficacy. | **MA** |
| 10 | **Use autonomy-supportive wording, and end by handing the choice back.** Avoid "must", "never", "you're harming your child" and exclamation marks. Prefer "many of us decided…", "you know your child", "if it suits you…". | Ma, Ebesu & Dillard (Journal of Communication, 35 studies, N = 10,658): freedom-threatening language → perceived threat → reactance (anger and counter-arguing) → less persuasion. Rains 2013: reactance is anger plus counter-arguing. Miller et al. 2007: a closing line that restores freedom improved outcomes. | **MA** + EXP |
| 11 | **Never shame parents whose child already has a phone.** Give them an action that applies to them too, such as "phones out of bedrooms at night". | Guilt appeals have small effects (g = 0.19) and also produce anger (r = .24 in an earlier meta-analysis). Messages that push repair can feel coercive. | **MA** |
| 12 | **Show the hidden majority, and never broadcast the "everyone has one" norm.** Say what parents privately want, and use local numbers once you have them. | Petrified Forest: a sign saying many visitors take wood increased theft. Schultz et al. 2007: descriptive norms can "boomerang" unless paired with approval. Pluralistic ignorance: people wrongly assume peers are more relaxed than they are (Prentice & Miller 1993). Harris Poll 2025: 66% of US parents want to delay smartphones until about 14, and 39% of parents who gave one wish they hadn't. | EXP (field) + MA (Rhodes et al. 2020) + SURVEY |
| 13 | **Present the pact as solving a coordination problem, with a visible threshold per grade, and show signatures growing.** For example: "It switches on when X families in your grade sign." Expect the "trend" message to have only a small effect on its own. The threshold and a public commitment do more of the work. | Bursztyn et al.: many users would pay for *everyone* to quit TikTok or Instagram (a "collective trap"). Fischbacher et al. 2001: about 50% of people are conditional cooperators. Centola et al. 2018: a 25% committed minority tipped a group convention. Veramendi 2026 (IFS working paper): schools where at least 20% of families signed the UK Parent Pact saw about 8% less recorded mental-health need. Lokhorst et al. 2013: commitment-making changes behaviour. Dynamic norms: meta-analysis r = .03-.10, with failed replications. | EXP + QE + MA |
| 14 | **Use one number plus one tiny story.** Use the statistic to shift belief and a 5-second moment from one family to shift intention. | Zebregs et al. 2015: statistical evidence moved beliefs and attitudes more, while narrative moved intentions more. Braddock & Dillard 2016: narratives have small positive effects (r about .17-.23). Shen et al. 2015: narrative effects held for audio and video but not for print. | **MA** |
| 15 | **Show the source on screen (researcher or journal and year), keep the full reference in the caption or on the site, and run it as a recognisable series.** Use the same opener and structure and the same closing line, "עוד קצת ילדות", in every episode. | Proof News / Sciences Po 2026: a randomised test (2,000 US adults) found source labels on short news videos increased trust. Pornpitakpan 2004: credible sources generally persuade more. Dechêne et al. 2010: repeated statements are judged truer (d = .39 to .50). Schmidt & Eisend 2015: repetition helps recall and attitude. | EXP (one study) + review + **MA** |

**What not to worry about.** Gain framing ("more sleep, more childhood") and loss framing ("lost sleep") barely differ in persuasiveness: O'Keefe & Jensen 2007 found r = .03 for prevention behaviours (**MA**). Pick whichever fits the warm tone.

**Light humour is fine for attention.** Keep the fact itself serious: humour raises attention but lowers perceived source credibility (Eisend 2009, **MA**).

### A worked timeline for one 30-second episode (applies rules 1-15)

| Time | What happens | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | Frame 1 shows big text plus moving collage: the fact, or a question about *your child* that is answered by second 3. The narrator says the same line. | 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| 3-8 s | The number, with an on-screen source tag (for example "מחקר, 2024"; the exact study to be confirmed per episode). | 14, 15 |
| 8-20 s | A tiny story: one child, one evening, drawn in the notebook. One visual change every 2-4 s, each tied to the words. | 6, 7, 14 |
| 20-27 s | One easy action for tonight, phrased as a choice. | 9, 10, 11 |
| 27-32 s | What most parents here want, the pact counter for the grade, a small campaign mark and the series line. | 8, 12, 13, 15 |

## (b) The evidence by question

### Q1. What makes people start and keep watching short-form video

#### 1.1 The first seconds matter most, but the exact number is not known

- **TikTok, "Auction Ads on TikTok: 9 Creative Tips"** (undated PDF): "over 63% of all videos with the highest click-through rate (CTR) highlight their key message or product within the first 3 seconds." Strength: **PD**, descriptive only. There is no comparison group, so we do not know what share of *all* videos do this. https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/Auction_Ads_Creative_Tips.pdf
- **TikTok for Business, "Video Advertising Guide"** (undated web page): "hook viewers in three seconds"; "Strong openings get higher watch-through rates"; "Skip long intros or logo animations". Strength: **EO / platform guidance**, with no data shown. https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/guides/video-advertising-guide
- **Meta / Toluna, "Deconstructing the power of Reels"** (Meta for Business, published 2 December 2025; Toluna research run in 2024). Method, per ppc.land: 100 Instagram Reels ads coded on 65 creative variables, each shown to 100 people in a simulated Reels feed in Germany, the UK, Italy and Turkey. Showing brand **and main message within the first 5 s** made an ad **1.7x** more likely to rank in the top 20% for purchase intent. A hook using both visuals and sound in the first 1-2 s gave **1.5x**. Strength: **PD**. These are associations from a small sample of ads, measuring purchase intent rather than behaviour. https://www.facebook.com/business/news/reels-creative-strategies ; method details: https://ppc.land/meta-and-toluna-study-finds-emojis-lift-reels-purchase-intent-2-5x/
- **Facebook IQ, "Capturing Attention in Feed"** (20 April 2016): people spend on average **1.7 s** on a piece of mobile-feed content vs 2.5 s on desktop (Facebook data). Fors Marsh Group found that "it takes only 0.25 seconds of exposure for people to recall mobile feed content at a statistically significant rate". Strength: **PD**. The 0.25 s finding is recognition of having seen something, not understanding it, and the Fors Marsh report is not public. https://www.facebook.com/business/news/insights/capturing-attention-feed-video-creative
- **Recommender-system research** treats a quick skip as a strong negative signal. For example, Lee, Park & Lee (AAAI 2025) label skips that happen "within a short time" as negatives. This supports the idea that early drop-off is what the algorithm punishes, but I could not confirm the commonly quoted "most skips happen in the first 5 s" from the abstract. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03107

**Bottom line.** Every platform says to put the point at the very start. The 3-second figure is a convention backed by correlational platform data, not by an experiment that compared 2 s with 3 s with 5 s.

#### 1.2 Early vs late branding

- **Google / YouTube Insights, "The First 5 Seconds"** (June 2015). Thousands of TrueView ads in 16 countries were coded on 170 attributes. Ads with the brand in the first 5 s had **higher ad recall and awareness lift, but people were more likely to skip them**. "Smiling characters" and recognisable faces in the first 5 s went with better viewership and brand lift. **Humour** was associated with both longer viewing and higher recall. Strength: **PD**, correlational. https://thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/1076/creating-youtube-ads-that-break-through-in-a-skippable-world_YcGhCSk.pdf
- **Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters (2010)**, "Moment-to-moment optimal branding in TV commercials: Preventing avoidance by pulsing", *Marketing Science* 29(5): 783-804. The study used eye-tracking and zapping data on 31 commercials with nearly 2,000 participants, plus an experiment with edited ads. Spreading the same total brand time into **brief, repeated appearances ("pulsing") reduced zapping**. Strength: **EXP + model**. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:29:y:2010:i:5:p:783-804
- **Meta / Toluna (above)**: showing the brand more than once across scenes → 1.8x. For direct-response ads, brand on screen for no more than 25% of the runtime → 4.8x. Strength: **PD**.
- Later Google "Unskippable Labs" case studies disagree with each other. One test (Maruti) found no view-through penalty for early branding. In a Wolt Shorts test, delayed branding cost 15% less per view, while early branding reached 75% more unique users with the brand. Strength: **AN** (single brand case studies). https://thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-apac/marketing-strategies/video/unskippable-labs-how-much-branding-youtube-too-much

**For us.** Our "brand" is the message, and that goes first. The campaign mark should be small, recurring and never an intro card.

#### 1.3 Captions and sound-off viewing

- **Facebook for Business, "Updated features for better video ads"** (undated page; press coverage dates it to February 2016). Internal tests found captioned video ads increased view time by **12%** on average. **41%** of video ads were "basically meaningless without sound", and "most video ads in mobile feed are viewed without sound". When feed ads play loudly and unexpectedly, **80%** of people react negatively. Strength: **PD**. https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads
- **Verizon Media & Publicis Media (2019)**: an online survey of 5,616 US adults aged 18-54 (April 2019). **69%** said they watch video with the sound off in public places, and about 25% do so in private. 37% said captions encourage them to turn the sound on. Strength: **SURVEY** (self-report). https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/verizon-media-and-publicis-media-find-viewers-want-captions/
- **TikTok / Kantar (January 2021)**: **88%** of TikTok users said sound is essential to the TikTok experience, and 73% said they would "stop and look" at TikTok ads with audio. Strength: **SURVEY** commissioned by the platform. https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/kantar-report-how-brands-are-making-noise-and-driving-impact-with-sound-on-tiktok
- **Instagram (Mosseri, 31 May 2023)**: *muted* reels are among those made less visible. Strength: **PD / official statement**. https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained
- **Gernsbacher (2015)**, "Video captions benefit everyone", *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 2(1): 195-202. This review of more than 100 studies found captions aid comprehension, attention and memory. Critics note that much of that evidence comes from second-language viewers, students and people who are deaf or hard of hearing. Strength: **review**. https://successforkidswithhearingloss.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Video-Captions-Benefit-Everyone_2015.pdf

**Bottom line.** Facebook feed and WhatsApp are often watched silently, while Reels and TikTok are sound-on platforms. Burned-in captions and a strong voice are both needed. There is no reliable single "X% watch muted" number for 2026.

#### 1.4 Faces and people

- Google 2015 (above): smiling characters and recognisable faces early → more viewing (**PD**).
- TikTok creative tips: "33% [of] auction ads with the highest VTR break the 4th wall", meaning they address the viewer directly (**PD**, descriptive).
- Meta / Toluna: "slice of life" scenes with people in everyday activities → 1.5x (**PD**).
- I found **no experiment** testing whether a face in the *first frame* of a short video raises watch time. Eye-tracking work shows faces attract gaze in video, but that is attention, not retention.

#### 1.5 Motion, cuts and "pattern interrupts"

- **Lang (2000)**, "The limited capacity model of mediated message processing", *Journal of Communication* 50(1). Viewers have limited processing resources. *Structural features* such as cuts and edits trigger automatic orienting responses that pull resources to the screen. Lang's pacing studies found that **cuts unrelated to the content are poorly encoded**, especially at fast pace. Strength: **EXP (lab studies) + theory**. I verified the model and these findings through secondary summaries, not the individual 1999-2000 papers. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP
- "Pattern interrupt" as a term comes from marketing practice. I found no study testing it as such in short video. Strength: **EO**.

#### 1.6 Text on screen

- TikTok creative tips: "40% of auction ads with the highest VTR" use text overlays (**PD**, descriptive).
- Instagram warns that **"mostly-text"** reels, watermarked reels and reels with borders are made less visible (Mosseri 2023, **PD**). Text should support the visuals, not replace them. A collage notebook with short handwritten-style lines is fine. A wall of text is not.

#### 1.7 Length, completion, shares and loops

- TikTok: "Up to 60 seconds, though 21-34 seconds performs best" (**platform guidance**, no data shown).
- Instagram, 2023: "The most important predictions we make are how likely you are to reshare a reel, watch a reel all the way through, like it, and go to the audio page" (**PD / official**). In January 2025 Mosseri named watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach as the top signals. I saw this only in secondary coverage. https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/
- **Loops** (an ending that flows back into the opening so people rewatch): I found **no published platform or academic data**. It is creator folklore (**AN**). Treat it as optional.

#### 1.8 Curiosity gap (Loewenstein 1994)

- **Loewenstein (1994)**, "The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation", *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1): 75-98. Curiosity arises when attention is drawn to a *gap* in one's knowledge. It is intense but **transient**, linked to impulsivity, and **tends to disappoint when satisfied**. Strength: **theory / review**. https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/
- **Kang et al. (2009)**, *Psychological Science* 20(8): higher curiosity about trivia answers predicted better recall of the answers 1-2 weeks later (**EXP**). https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html
- **Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014)**, *Neuron* 84(2): 486-496: in a high-curiosity state, people also remembered *unrelated* material better, immediately and a day later (**EXP**). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494

**For us.** A real question the parent wants answered ("how much sleep does a phone in the room cost?") helps memory of the answer. The answer must come quickly and be worth it (see Q2).

### Q2. Hooks

| Hook type | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Question aimed at the viewer** | Lai & Farbrot (2014), *Social Influence* 9(4): 289-299. On a Norwegian science Twitter feed (about 6,350 followers), self-referencing question headlines got about **175%** more clicks than statements and other questions about 150%. In product ads on an e-commerce site, self-referencing questions got 257% more. The authors warned the context was limited (**EXP, field, small**). https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/are-you-more-likely-click-headlines-are-phrased-question | Works for clicks, but see the next row |
| **Question / forward-reference ("curiosity gap") headlines** | Scacco & Muddiman (2019/2020), "The curiosity effect", *New Media & Society*, DOI 10.1177/1461444819863408. Traditional summary headlines beat curiosity headlines in a lab study and in selection data from nine local news sites (**EXP + field**). The same team's 2016 Engaging News Project report found question headlines led to *lower* expected engagement. https://www.journalism.co.uk/readers-perceive-question-based-headlines-more-negatively-study-shows/ A later NSF-funded study found headline format did not change story selection, but question headlines were judged **least credible**. https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10191439-headline-format-influences-evaluation-engagement-environmental-news | Mixed. Withholding the answer costs trust. |
| **Negative / surprising wording** | Robertson et al. (2023), *Nature Human Behaviour* 7: 812-822. Across 12,448 Upworthy A/B tests (about 105,000 headlines in the full dataset), each **negative word raised CTR by 2.3%** and each positive word lowered it by 1.0%. **Sadness** words raised clicks (+0.7% per SD), while **fear words lowered them (-0.7%)** and anger had no effect (**EXP, very large**). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/ | Concern and a gentle sense of loss pull people in. Scary words do not. |
| **Industry "hook rules" in general** | Banerjee & Urminsky (2025), *Marketing Science* 44(3): 566-592, analysed thousands of Upworthy experiments. Textual cues recommended by prior research do matter, "but the prior research does not provide useful guidance as to the direction of the effects" (in the 2021 working paper). **EXP, large**. https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v44y2025i3p566-592.html | Test, don't trust rules of thumb |
| **Surprising number** | No direct test in short video. Statistics move *beliefs* more than stories do (Zebregs et al. 2015, **MA**). | Reasonable, if the number is concrete and sourced |
| **Direct address / looking at camera** | TikTok: 33% of top-VTR ads break the 4th wall (**PD**). | Plausible; correlational only |
| **Visual surprise** | Orienting-response research (Lang 2000) supports novel, sudden visual change grabbing attention (**EXP, lab**). No short-video test of "visual surprise hooks" was found. | Plausible |
| **Asking parents about their own plan** | The "question-behaviour effect": asking people to predict their own behaviour slightly increases doing it. Wood et al. (2016), *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 20(3): 245-268, 116 tests, small positive effect, larger for desirable and easy behaviours (**MA**). https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/88297/ | Useful for the pact episode: "When are *you* planning the first phone?" |

**How fast must the payoff come?** No study gives a number for short video. The only data points are platform correlations: key message by 3 s (TikTok) and brand plus message by 5 s (Meta/Toluna). Loewenstein's account says curiosity fades fast and disappoints when it is resolved badly. The headline studies show that an unanswered gap lowers credibility. A practical reading is to answer the hook question within about 2-4 s, then spend the rest of the video on *why* and *what to do*. This is an inference, not a measured threshold.

### Q3. Persuading parents without backlash

#### 3.1 Fear appeals need an action step

- **Tannenbaum et al. (2015)**, "Appealing to fear", *Psychological Bulletin* 141(6): 1178-1204. The meta-analysis covered 127 articles and 248 samples, N = 27,372. Overall effect on attitudes, intentions and behaviours: **d = 0.29**. Effects were larger when the message **included efficacy statements** (95% CI [0.31, 0.55] vs [0.13, 0.29] without), showed high susceptibility and severity, recommended **one-time** behaviours ([0.30, 0.56] vs [0.14, 0.29] for repeated ones), and when audiences were more female. Each extra 10% women added about d = 0.03. The authors found "no identified circumstances under which they backfire". Strength: **MA**. https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2021-07/e3eff4_f9429b1d5e644365afa9ba68ba35298c.pdf (DOI 10.1037/a0039729)
- **Witte & Allen (2000)**, *Health Education & Behavior* 27(5): 591-615 (the EPPM meta-analysis). Strong fear with high efficacy produced the most behaviour change. Strong fear with **low efficacy produced the most defensive responses** (avoidance, reactance). Strength: **MA**. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11009129/
- **Peters, Ruiter & Kok (2013)**, *Health Psychology Review* 7(Suppl 1): 8-31. Only 6 studies fully crossed threat with efficacy and measured behaviour. In them, threat helped only under high efficacy (d = 0.31) and efficacy helped only under high threat (d = 0.71). I took these numbers from an abstract listing. Strength: **MA (small)**. https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/threatening-communication-a-critical-re-analysis-and-a-revised-me

**For us.** Stating a harm clearly is fine. It does not need to be graphic, and our audience is defensive, so dramatising adds risk without much gain. Every episode needs one doable step. "Sign the pact" is a one-time behaviour, which is the kind fear appeals work best for.

#### 3.2 Reactance and controlling language

- **Ma, Ebesu & Dillard** (*Journal of Communication*, DOI 10.1093/joc/jqaf004; covered January 2026). The meta-analysis covered 35 studies, N = 10,658, all on personal health behaviours. Freedom-threatening language (imperatives such as "must" or "have to", absolute claims, exclamation marks, removing choice) → perceived threat → **state reactance** → less agreement and lower intentions. Simply counting threatening features did not predict how strong the reaction would be. Strength: **MA**. I read a detailed summary, not the paper. https://www.psypost.org/forceful-language-makes-people-resist-health-advice/
- **Rains (2013)**, *Human Communication Research* 39(1): 47-73. K = 20 studies, N = 4,942. Reactance is best modelled as intertwined **anger and counter-arguing**. Strength: **MA**. https://academic.oup.com/hcr/article-abstract/39/1/47/4093682
- **Miller et al. (2007)**, *Human Communication Research* 33(2): 219-240. Controlling language had negative effects. A **restoration postscript** (a closing line that reminds people the choice is theirs) produced more favourable outcomes. Concrete wording drew more attention and made the source look better. Strength: **EXP**. DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2958.2007.00297.x
- **"But you are free" technique.** Carpenter (2013, *Communication Studies* 64: 6-17) reported it boosts compliance. A later pre-registered meta-analysis (Fillon et al., *Metapsychology*) found g = 0.44 overall, but **no effect (g = 0.11, CI crosses 0)** in the 7 low-risk-of-bias studies. Strength: **MA, contested**. https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2640 Using the phrase does no harm, but it will not carry the message on its own.

#### 3.3 Guilt and shame

- **Peng et al. (2023)**, "When guilt works: a comprehensive meta-analysis of guilt appeals", *Frontiers in Psychology* (DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1201631). Small overall effect, **g = 0.19**. Messages that push reparative action can make people feel coerced. A 2021 meta-analysis they cite found guilt appeals also produce **anger (r = .24, k = 8)**. Strength: **MA**. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568480/

**For us.** Many parents in the audience already gave a phone, or will soon. Give them a role, such as night-time charging outside the bedroom, instead of blame.

#### 3.4 Social norms

- **Petrified Forest field experiment.** Cialdini et al. (2006), *Social Influence* 1(1): 3-15; Cialdini (2003), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 12(4): 105-109. Signs stressing that many visitors steal wood (a descriptive norm) **increased** theft. Signs stressing disapproval (an injunctive norm) reduced it. Secondary sources give about 7.92% vs 1.67% of marked pieces stolen; I did not see the table myself. Strength: **EXP (field)**. https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/45277
- **Schultz et al. (2007)**, *Psychological Science* 18(5): 429-434. Telling households the neighbourhood average cut use among high users but made low users use *more* (a boomerang). Adding approval (a smiley) removed the boomerang. Strength: **EXP (field)**. https://cbsm.com/articles/32578-the-constructive-destructive-and-reconstructive-power-of-social-norms
- **Rhodes, Shulman & McClaran (2020)**, "Changing norms", *Human Communication Research* 46(2-3): 161-191. The meta-analysis of 110 articles found injunctive norms "may be more effective in changing behavior than previously considered". Strength: **MA**. I read the abstract only. DOI 10.1093/hcr/hqz023
- **Pluralistic ignorance.** Prentice & Miller (1993), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 64: 243-256. Students believed peers were more comfortable with campus drinking than peers actually were, and men drifted toward the imagined norm. Strength: **EXP / survey series**. https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/pluralistic-ignorance-alcohol-use-campus-some-consequences
- **What parents actually think.** These are US and UK polls; no Israeli equivalent was found.
  - Harris Poll (21-27 February 2025, 1,013 US parents, run with Jonathan Haidt's team): **66%** would like to restrict smartphones until high school (about 14). **39%** of parents who gave a smartphone "wish they hadn't but they felt that they had to give in because so many of their children's friends already had one". 54% felt they had to allow social media because other families had. Strength: **SURVEY**. https://theharrispoll.com/briefs/what-parents-think-about-their-kids-social-media-and-smartphone-usage/
  - Parentkind (19 February - 4 March 2024, 2,496 parents in England): 58% support banning smartphones for under-16s, rising to **77% among parents of primary-school children** and 84% among parents who have not yet given a phone. Strength: **SURVEY**. https://www.parentkind.org.uk/research-and-policy/parent-research/research-library/school-education-policies/parent-poll-on-smartphones-march-2024
- **Dynamic ("trending") norms.**
  - Sparkman & Walton (2017), *Psychological Science* 28(11): 1663-1674. Telling people that more and more others are changing increased interest in eating less meat. In a café, meatless orders roughly doubled (about 17-21% → 34%; the exact control figure differs between secondary sources). Strength: **EXP**. DOI 10.1177/0956797617719950
  - **However**, a 2026 meta-analysis (Reintgen Kamphuisen, Bouman & van der Werff, *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 79 studies) found **very small effects (r = .03-.10)**. Effects were larger with general reference groups, short timeframes, and a combined descriptive-plus-injunctive message. https://epgroningen.nl/unpacking-dynamic-norms-identifying-key-elements-of-dynamic-norms-and-a-meta-analysis-of-their-effects-on-pro-environmental-behaviour/ (DOI 10.1016/j.jenvp.2026.103060)
  - Direct tests often found nothing, for example Aldoh, Sparks & Harris (2024), *Royal Society Open Science*, DOI 10.1098/rsos.240407. Strength: **MA → small**.

#### 3.5 Gain vs loss framing

- **O'Keefe & Jensen (2007)**, *Journal of Health Communication* 12(7): 623-644. 93 studies, N = 21,656. For prevention behaviours, gain frames were "significantly" more persuasive, but the difference was tiny (**r = .03**) and driven by dental-hygiene studies. Strength: **MA**.
- **O'Keefe & Jensen (2009)**, *Journal of Communication* 59(2): 296-316. 53 studies, N = 9,145. For detection behaviours there was no meaningful advantage overall; loss frames had a small edge only for breast-cancer detection. Strength: **MA**. https://www.cbsm.com/articles/33455-the-relative-persuasiveness-of-gain-framed-and-loss-framed-messages-for-encouraging-disease-detectio

**Conclusion.** Framing choice is a matter of tone, not effectiveness.

#### 3.6 Narrative vs statistics

- **Braddock & Dillard (2016)**, *Communication Monographs* 83: 446-467. 74 studies. Narratives had small positive effects on beliefs (r ≈ .17), attitudes (r ≈ .19) and behaviours (r ≈ .23; the behaviour figure comes from only 5 studies). Secondary summaries disagree about moderators such as fiction vs non-fiction, so I don't rely on them. Strength: **MA**, figures via secondary sources. https://mediawell.ssrc.org/?p=52168
- **Shen, Sheer & Li (2015)**, *Journal of Advertising* 44(2): 105-113. 25 health studies, N = 9,330. Overall effect r = .063. **Audio and video narratives were effective, print was not**. Narratives worked for prevention and detection but not for cessation. Strength: **MA**.
- **Zebregs et al. (2015)**, *Health Communication* 30(3): 282-289. **Statistics** moved beliefs and attitudes more, while **narratives** moved intention more. Strength: **MA**. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24836931/

**For us.** One sourced number plus a 5-10 second micro-story of a child and a parent is the evidence-backed combination.

#### 3.7 Source credibility and on-screen citations

- **Pornpitakpan (2004)**, *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* 34(2): 243-281. This review of five decades of studies finds high-credibility sources generally persuade more, with some exceptions. Strength: **review**.
- **Proof News & Sciences Po (reported 27 October 2026)**. In a randomised survey experiment, 2,000 US adults each saw one short-form news video. **Source labels increased trust**. A sources label in the video *description* had the strongest effect, and an end-card or credits label had a slightly smaller one. No effect sizes were published. The study covered news, not health. Strength: **EXP (one study)**. https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/10/creators-who-show-their-sources-build-trust-survey-finds/
- **Eisend (2009)**, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* 37. Humour increases attention and liking of the ad but **lowers perceived source credibility**. Strength: **MA**. DOI 10.1007/s11747-008-0096-y

**Local narrator.** I found no study on "a familiar local voice vs an expert" for parenting messages. Using a neighbour fits "trusted messenger" practice, which counts as **EO**. Pair the neighbour's voice with a named scientific source on screen, so the credibility comes from the research and the warmth from the voice.

#### 3.8 Repetition and series effects

- **Dechêne et al. (2010)**, "The truth about the truth", *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 14(2): 238-257. 51 studies. Repeated statements are rated truer: **d = .39** within items and **.50** compared with new items. Strength: **MA**. Later reviewers note the coded data are not available. DOI 10.1177/1088868309352251
- **Schmidt & Eisend (2015)**, *Journal of Advertising* 44(4): 415-428. Meta-analysis on advertising repetition. Secondary summaries say attitude peaks at around **10 exposures** and recall keeps rising past 8. I could not verify these numbers in the paper. Strength: **MA**, numbers unverified.
- **Teixeira et al. 2010 (pulsing)** and **Meta/Toluna "brand more than once"** support brief, recurring brand moments.

**For us.** Eight episodes that repeat one core line, "עוד קצת ילדות", and one visual signature can build both familiarity and believability without the same video being seen ten times. Use repetition only for true claims.

#### 3.9 Collective action, pledges and the pact

- **Bursztyn, Handel, Jiménez-Durán & Roth**, "When product markets become collective traps: The case of social media" (working paper, November 2024). In incentivised experiments with more than 1,000 US college students, many users would have to be paid to quit alone, yet many would **pay to have everyone deactivate TikTok or Instagram**. About 57-58% said they would prefer to live in a world without these apps (figures via secondary coverage). Strength: **EXP**, working paper. https://home.uchicago.edu/bursztyn/CollectiveTraps_Nov2024.pdf
- **Fischbacher, Gächter & Fehr (2001)**, *Economics Letters* 71(3): 397-404. About **50%** of people are conditional cooperators who contribute if others do, and about a third free-ride. Replicated in several countries. Strength: **EXP**.
- **Centola et al. (2018)**, *Science* 360: 1116-1119. In 10 groups of 20 people, committed minorities of 21% or less failed to overturn a convention, while **25%** succeeded. Critics note participants were paid to coordinate and other studies find higher thresholds. Strength: **EXP**. DOI 10.1126/science.aas8827
- **Lokhorst et al. (2013)**, *Environment and Behavior* 45(1): 3-34. Commitment-making changed behaviour in the short and long term, compared with controls. Strength: **MA** (environmental behaviours). DOI 10.1177/0013916511411477
- **Pledges can stop working when they become too common.** Bearman & Brückner (2001), *American Journal of Sociology* 106(4): 859-912, found virginity pledges delayed first sex only where *some but not too many* students pledged. The NIH press release puts the cutoff at about 30%. A later matched-comparison study found no effect. Strength: **observational**. The mechanism there was a minority identity, which differs from a coordination pact, but it is a reminder that signing is not doing. https://www.nichd.nih.gov/newsroom/releases/virginity

**Evaluated screen-time and parenting campaigns**

| Campaign | Evaluation? |
|---|---|
| **Smartphone Free Childhood (UK Parent Pact, wait until 14)** | **Yes, a quasi-experiment.** Veramendi (Royal Holloway), IFS Working Paper 26/45, July 2026, not peer-reviewed. Matched difference-in-differences comparing English primary schools with ≥20% of families signed against schools with <5%. School-recorded social, emotional and mental-health need fell by about **8%**, and child psychiatric disability claims by about **6%**. The effect was already present at a 10% sign-up rate, rose with uptake, and was larger among younger children. The 20% threshold was chosen because at one school (Cunningham Hill) about 38-40% registered, yet Year 6 phone ownership fell from 75% to 12%. The author concludes the tipping point is "somewhere below 40 percent". Caveats from the author: engaged parents may differ, the estimates are intention-to-treat, and the follow-up is short-run. SFC supplied the sign-up data. By early 2026 more than 165,000 parents at more than 15,600 schools had signed. https://ifs.org.uk/publications/smartphones-and-childrens-mental-health (PDF: https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2026-07/WP202645-Smartphones-and-childrens-mental-health.pdf) |
| **Wait Until 8th (US)** | **No independent evaluation found.** The design detail: a pledge "is active" once **10 families in the same grade at a school** sign, and families are then told who else signed. https://www.waituntil8th.org/blog/2024/2/29/how-can-you-rally-other-families-to-delay-the-smartphone-with-you |
| **AAP Family Media Plan** | **RCT, null on the main outcome.** Moreno et al. (2021), *JAMA Pediatrics*: 1,520 parent-adolescent pairs. Most made a plan, but there was no significant change in adolescents' engagement with media rules. https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2775421 |

**For us.**
- A per-grade threshold that switches the pact on, as Wait Until 8th does, turns "I'll wait if others wait" into a visible, safe commitment.
- The UK evidence suggests about 20% sign-up per school or grade is where measurable change begins, and about 25-40% is where ownership can tip. The plan's 30% target sits sensibly inside that range.
- Signing is not the outcome. Keep episodes after the pact focused on what signed families actually do.

## (c) Myths and weak claims to avoid repeating

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| "85% of Facebook videos are watched without sound" | **Outdated and weak.** It comes from a Digiday article (May 2016) quoting a few publishers' own figures (LittleThings, Mic) and an agency's client average, for pre-Reels Facebook feed video. It was never an independent measurement. Reels and TikTok are sound-on platforms (TikTok/Kantar 2021; Instagram demotes muted reels). https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/ |
| "People's attention span is 8 seconds, less than a goldfish" | **Myth.** It appeared in a 2015 Microsoft Canada consumer report, which cited "Statistic Brain", a site with no traceable research behind the figure. BBC's 2017 investigation found no source. Attention depends on the task; there is no fixed "average attention span". https://www.bbc.com/news/health-38896790 |
| "47% of a video's value is delivered in the first 3 seconds (74% by 10 s)" | **Unverifiable.** It is attributed to a 2015 Facebook-commissioned Nielsen study. I could not find the original, and secondary sources disagree on whether it means "share of value" or "ad-recall lift". Don't cite it. |
| "Captions make people 80% more likely to finish a video" | **Unverified.** It is attributed to Verizon/Publicis 2019, but the original wording was not found. That survey's verified figures are 69% sound-off in public and 37% saying captions make them turn sound on. |
| "Facebook found captions add 12% view time" | **Real but narrow.** Facebook's internal 2016 test on feed video ads. It is platform data, not independent, and from the sound-off feed era. Fine to use as background only. |
| "TikTok ads of 21-34 s get 280% more conversions" | **Unverified.** The 21-34 s recommendation is TikTok's, but the 280% figure has no primary source. |
| "Instagram pushes reels that are watched past 25%" / "sends are worth 3-5x likes" | **Unverified marketing-blog claims.** Instagram's own statements name reshares, watch-to-the-end, likes and audio-page visits (2023) and watch time, likes and sends (2025), with no thresholds or weights. |
| "Fear appeals backfire" | **Not supported** by the largest meta-analysis (Tannenbaum 2015: no backfire found). The real lesson is that fear *without an action step* is weaker and can trigger defensiveness (Witte & Allen 2000). |
| "Scarier = more clicks" | **Not supported.** In about 12,000 Upworthy experiments, fear words *lowered* click-through. Sadness words raised it (Robertson et al. 2023). |
| "Gain framing works better for prevention" | **Trivially small** (r = .03), and driven by one topic (O'Keefe & Jensen 2007). |
| "Show that norms are changing and behaviour doubles" | **Overstated.** It rests on one café study. A 2026 meta-analysis found r = .03-.10, and several direct tests found nothing. |
| "Question headlines get 175% more clicks" | **One small 2014 Twitter study.** Other experiments found question and curiosity-gap headlines less credible and no better at driving selection. |
| "'But you are free' doubles compliance" | **Contested.** No effect in the low-bias studies of a pre-registered meta-analysis. |
| "Stories always beat statistics" | **Depends on the goal.** Statistics move beliefs more; narratives move intentions more (Zebregs et al. 2015). Narrative effects are small on average. |
| "Pledges like Wait Until 8th are proven to work" | **No evaluation of Wait Until 8th exists.** The only causal-style evidence is the UK Parent Pact working paper (QE, not yet peer-reviewed). The AAP Family Media Plan RCT was null. |
| "Most kids already have a phone, so…" (as a campaign line) | **Harmful framing.** Advertising the problem norm can increase the behaviour (Petrified Forest; Schultz 2007). Lead with what parents want and what they are doing together. |

## (d) Open questions (no good evidence found)

1. **WhatsApp.** No platform or academic data on what makes a video watched or forwarded in WhatsApp groups or Status. This is probably our main channel in a small town. Measure it ourselves, for example with a short tracked link per episode and asking in class groups.
2. **Hebrew and Israeli parents.** All the platform data is from the US and Europe, and the surveys are US and UK. No Israeli study on parents' smartphone norms or pluralistic ignorance was found. A short anonymous local survey ("When would *you* like to give the first phone? When do you think *other parents* will?") would give us honest local norm data for rules 12-13. It would also be the hidden-majority message itself.
3. **Illustrated collage vs live action.** No study compares hand-made or illustrated styles with live footage for retention or credibility in short video. TikTok advises balancing "authentic-looking" with polished. Consider mixing in real local parents' faces, at least in the pact episode.
4. **Exact payoff timing.** 3 s and 5 s are platform conventions from correlational data. No experiment fixes the threshold.
5. **Loops and rewatches.** No published evidence either way.
6. **On-screen citations in health short video.** Only one news-video experiment, which found the label in the *description* worked best. Whether a 1-second on-screen source tag helps, or distracts, in a 30-second Hebrew video is untested.
7. **Effective pact threshold for a small Israeli town.** UK evidence points to about 20% for a measurable effect and about 25-40% for a tipping point, but grade sizes in our school are small. With 25 families a grade, 20% is 5 families. Whether a threshold that small holds up socially is unknown.
8. **Does signing translate into delay?** Pledge research shows signing and doing can diverge. Plan a simple follow-up, such as an anonymous "did we keep it?" check at the end of the school year.

**Suggested low-cost testing.** For each episode, make two first-3-second versions (for example, the fact stated vs a question answered immediately). Post them to similar audiences, such as two class WhatsApp groups or Instagram trial posts, and compare 3-second hold, average % watched, completion and forwards/sends. Banerjee & Urminsky's finding that rules of thumb often get the *direction* wrong is a good reason to test rather than assume.
