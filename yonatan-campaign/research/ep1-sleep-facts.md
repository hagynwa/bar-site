# Episode 1: Phones in the bedroom at night and children's sleep. Fact check

Prepared 2026-11-18. All numbers below were checked against the PubMed record (abstract) and, where marked, the full text. Nothing here is estimated or paraphrased from memory. Where a source could not be opened, this is stated.

## 1. Recommendation for the video

### MAIN FACT (one number)

**On-screen wording (English master):**

> **Children who use a phone or tablet at bedtime have more than double the odds of not getting enough sleep.**
>
> *Carter et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2016*

Optional small print (if there is room): *Review of 20 studies, 125,198 children aged 6–19. Shows a link, not proof of cause.*

- Source number: pooled odds ratio **2.17 (95% CI 1.42–3.32)** for bedtime device use and inadequate sleep quantity.
- Why this one: it is the most-cited meta-analysis on exactly this topic (portable devices at bedtime). It was published in a top paediatrics journal, it pools 125,198 children, and the number is simple (×2).
- Wording rules: say **"more than double the odds"** (or "more than twice the odds"). Do **not** say "twice as likely" or "2 times more children". See section 5 for why.

**Strong companion line (no new number needed, same source). Use it if the script is about the phone being in the room:**

> **Even when it wasn't being used, a phone or tablet in the bedroom at night was linked to less sleep.**
>
> *Carter et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2016*

(Odds ratio 1.79, 95% CI 1.39–2.31 for "access without use" and inadequate sleep quantity. If you need a number: "about 1.8 times the odds". Avoid "almost twice as likely".)

### BACKUP FACT (one number, closer to our children's age)

> **Children who slept near a phone or other small screen got about 20 minutes less sleep on school nights.**
>
> *Falbe et al., Pediatrics, 2015*

- Source number: **20.6 fewer minutes** of weekday sleep (95% CI 11.4–29.7 fewer). Based on 2,048 4th- and 7th-graders (roughly ages 9–13) in Massachusetts, USA. Cross-sectional.
- Why it is a good backup: the age range is close to ours, minutes are easy to understand, and no odds ratio has to be explained. Note that "small screen" in the study means smartphones, tablets and similar devices, compared with "never" sleeping near one. Strict wording would be "reported about 20 fewer minutes"; "got about 20 minutes less sleep" is acceptable in a 30-second video if the citation is shown.

### Useful context line (not a "finding", a guideline)

> **Children aged 6–12 need 9–12 hours of sleep a night.** *(American Academy of Sleep Medicine, 2016)*
> On-screen citation: *Paruthi et al., J Clin Sleep Med, 2016*

(For the 5-year-olds in the audience: ages 3–5 need 10–13 hours per 24 hours, including naps.)

And the official recommendation, if the video ends with a call to action:

> **Pediatricians recommend: no devices in the child's bedroom at night, and no screens in the hour before bed.**
> On-screen citation: *American Academy of Pediatrics, Pediatrics, 2016*

## 3. Details and exact quotes

### 3.1 Carter et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2016 (main source)

- **Citation:** Carter B, Rees P, Hale L, Bhattacharjee D, Paradkar MS. Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. *JAMA Pediatr.* 2016;170(12):1202–1208.
- **DOI:** 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341. **URL:** https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341 · PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27802500/ · Free author manuscript: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5380441/
- **Scope:** portable devices only (phones, tablets). TVs and desktop computers were excluded. Literature published 2011 to 15 June 2015, 12 databases, no language restriction.
- **Studies and participants:** the abstract says "Twenty studies were included" with **125,198 children (mean [SD] age, 14.5 [2.2] years; 50.1% male)**. The full text says 20 studies were *assessed for quality*, **17 were included**, and 3 were excluded for poor methods. The published Key Points and the abstract use "20". For the video, use "125,198 children" or "over 125,000 children", and "20 studies" only if you cite it as the abstract does. "About 20 studies" is safe.
- **Age:** inclusion 6–19 years. The authors say "the majority were aged between 10 and 18". **This is mainly an adolescent evidence base.** No age subgroup effect was found, but the 6–11 data were thin.
- **Definitions (full text):** *access* = bedtime access to a device at least 3 times a week. *Use* = used a device around bedtime. Inadequate sleep quantity = less than 10 h (children) or 9 h (adolescents) a day.
- **Studies per pooled estimate (full text):** use → sleep quantity: 7 studies. Use → daytime sleepiness: only 2 studies. Access → sleep quantity: 6 studies. Access → daytime sleepiness: 4 studies.
- **Heterogeneity:** use → sleep quantity I² = 90% (high). The authors traced this to one study (Chahal 2013, 10–11-year-olds only). Without it, OR = 2.52 (1.79–3.55).
- **Quality:** "The GRADE assessment of included studies was low, due to their non-randomized nature ... Therefore the level of evidence for all findings is low."

**Key abstract sentences (verbatim, published version):**

> "There was a strong and consistent association between bedtime media device use and inadequate sleep quantity (odds ratio [OR], 2.17; 95% CI, 1.42-3.32) (P < .001, I2 = 90%), poor sleep quality (OR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.14-1.88) (P = .003, I2 = 76%), and excessive daytime sleepiness (OR, 2.72; 95% CI, 1.32-5.61) (P = .007, I2 = 50%)."

> "In addition, children who had access to (but did not use) media devices at night were more likely to have inadequate sleep quantity (OR, 1.79; 95% CI, 1.39-2.31) (P < .001, I2 = 64%), poor sleep quality (OR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.11-2.10) (P = .009, I2 = 74%), and excessive daytime sleepiness (OR, 2.27; 95% CI, 1.54-3.35) (P < .001, I2 = 24%)."

> "Bedtime access to and use of a media device were significantly associated with the following: inadequate sleep quantity, poor sleep quality, and excessive daytime sleepiness. An integrated approach among teachers, health care professionals, and parents is required to minimize device access at bedtime ..."

From the full text: "Media device presence in the bedroom (even without use) was also associated with increased odds of detrimental sleep outcomes."

**Does access without use matter?** Yes, according to this review: all three access-without-use odds ratios are statistically significant (1.79, 1.53, 2.27). This supports the message "the phone does not need to be in the bedroom at all". It is still an association. Children allowed to keep a phone in the bedroom may also differ in other ways (family rules, age, bedtime).

A small discrepancy: the author manuscript gives the upper CI for OR 2.17 as 3.33. The published abstract says 3.32. Use the published 3.32.

**Plain-language phrasings (correct):**
- "Kids who used a phone or tablet at bedtime had more than double the odds of getting too little sleep."
- "Even when not used, having the device in the bedroom at night was linked to higher odds of too little sleep."
- "Bedtime phone use was linked to more than double the odds of being overly sleepy during the day." (OR 2.72, but only 2 studies, wide CI. **Not recommended as a headline.**)

### 3.2 Hale & Guan, Sleep Medicine Reviews 2015

- Hale L, Guan S. Screen time and sleep among school-aged children and adolescents: a systematic literature review. *Sleep Med Rev.* 2015;21:50–58. DOI 10.1016/j.smrv.2014.07.007. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25193149/
- Quote: "We reviewed 67 studies published from 1999 to early 2014. We found that screen time is adversely associated with sleep outcomes (primarily shortened duration and delayed timing) in 90% of studies."
- Limitation quote: "1) causal association not confirmed".
- Recommendation quote: "Youth should be advised to limit or reduce screen time exposure, especially before or during bedtime hours".
- Plain language: "9 out of 10 studies found that more screen time goes with less or later sleep." On-screen citation: *Hale & Guan, Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2015.* Caveat: this counts studies. It is not an effect size, and it covers all screens, not only phones in the bedroom.

### 3.3 Falbe et al., Pediatrics 2015 (backup)

- Falbe J, Davison KK, Franckle RL, et al. Sleep duration, restfulness, and screens in the sleep environment. *Pediatrics.* 2015;135(2):e367–e375. DOI 10.1542/peds.2014-2306. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25560435/
- Quote: "Children who slept near a small screen (compared with never) reported 20.6 fewer minutes of sleep (95% confidence interval [CI], -29.7 to -11.4) and had a higher prevalence of perceived insufficient rest or sleep (prevalence ratio, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.21 to 1.60)."
- Conclusion quote: "These findings caution against unrestricted screen access in children's bedrooms."
- Note: the 1.39 is a **prevalence ratio**, so "39% more likely to report not getting enough rest" is a legitimate phrasing (unlike an odds ratio). This study is one of those included in Carter 2016.

### 3.4 ABCD Study (Nagata et al.)

- Nagata JM, Singh G, Yang JH, et al. Bedtime screen use behaviors and sleep outcomes: Findings from the ABCD Study. *Sleep Health.* 2023;9(4):497–502. DOI 10.1016/j.sleh.2023.02.005. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10823798/
  - Quote: "Adolescents who had a television or an Internet-connected electronic device in the bedroom had a greater risk of having trouble falling or staying asleep (adjusted risk ratio 1.27, 95% CI 1.12-1.44) and overall sleep disturbance (adjusted risk ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.06-1.25)."
  - Full text: "The majority (63.2%) of early adolescents had a TV or electronic device in their bedroom". Also 20.0% used a device when they woke during the night, and 16.9% had been woken by calls, texts or emails at least once in the past week.
  - Plain language: "Kids aged 10–14 with a TV or internet device in the bedroom were about 27% more likely to have trouble falling or staying asleep." (Risk ratio, so "more likely" is correct.) Note that this lumps TV together with phones and tablets.
- Nagata JM, Cheng CM, Shim J, et al. Bedtime Screen Use Behaviors and Sleep Outcomes in Early Adolescents: A Prospective Cohort Study. *J Adolesc Health.* 2024;75(4):650–655. DOI 10.1016/j.jadohealth.2024.06.006. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11610308/
  - Quote: "Having a television or Internet-connected electronic device in the bedroom was prospectively associated with shorter sleep duration one year later."
  - Value of this study: it follows children forward in time (11–12 → 12–13), which is stronger than a snapshot. In this follow-up, a device in the bedroom was *not* significantly linked to caregiver-reported sleep disturbance (OR 1.08, 0.94–1.25), only to shorter sleep.

### 3.5 Objective measurement: Brosnan et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2024

- Brosnan B, Haszard JJ, Meredith-Jones KA, et al. Screen Use at Bedtime and Sleep Duration and Quality Among Youths. *JAMA Pediatr.* 2024;178(11):1147–1154. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2024.2914. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226046/
- Quote: "Every 10 minutes of additional screen time in bed was associated with shorter total sleep time (mean difference, -3 minutes; 95% CI, -6 to -1 minute)."
- Quote: "screen time once in bed was associated with impairment of sleep, especially when screen time was interactive or involved multitasking."
- Also: "These findings suggest that current sleep hygiene recommendations to restrict all screen time before bed seem neither achievable nor appropriate."
- Why it matters for us: the study supports the specific message "**not in bed / not in the bedroom**" more than "no screens in the evening at all". Small sample (79), ages 11–14.

Quote: "During one week of phone restriction, adolescents stopped using their phones earlier (80 min, p < .001), turned their lights off earlier (17 min, p = .01), and slept longer (21 min, p = .01)." ### 3.7 Mechanism

- **LeBourgeois MK, Hale L, Chang AM, et al.** Digital Media and Sleep in Childhood and Adolescence. *Pediatrics.* 2017;140(Suppl 2):S92–S96. DOI 10.1542/peds.2016-1758J. Quote: "The underlying mechanisms of these associations likely include the following: (1) time displacement (ie, time spent on screens replaces time spent sleeping and other activities); (2) psychological stimulation based on media content; and (3) the effects of light emitted from devices on circadian timing, sleep physiology, and alertness." Also: "limited by cross-sectional, observational, and self-reported data."
- **Higuchi S, Nagafuchi Y, Lee SI, Harada T.** Influence of light at night on melatonin suppression in children. *J Clin Endocrinol Metab.* 2014;99(9):3298–3303. DOI 10.1210/jc.2014-1629. Quote: "the percentage of melatonin suppression by light in children was almost twice that in adults". Caveats: room light (580 lux), not a phone screen. ### 3.10 Newer evidence that softens the message (know it, do not hide it)

- **Bourke M et al.** Within-Person Association Between Daily Screen Use and Sleep in Youth: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. *JAMA Pediatr.* 2026;180(5):500–509. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.6490. DOI 10.1186/s12889-021-11640-9. 49 studies, ages 0–15. Quote: "for 6-12-year-old children, there was evidence for associations of electronic media use with delayed bedtime and poor sleep quality." Also: "electronic media use was generally associated with shorter sleep duration in children and adolescents." This is useful as the age-specific (6–12) review backing.

## 4. How to phrase odds ratios honestly (recommendation)

An **odds ratio (OR)** compares *odds* (p / (1−p)), not chances. When the outcome is rare (under ~10%), OR ≈ "times as likely". When the outcome is common, the OR **overstates** the "times as likely" figure.

"Inadequate sleep" is common in these samples. The definition is less than 10 h for children and less than 9 h for teens; in the ABCD sample, 28% had caregiver-reported sleep disturbance. Carter et al. do not give a single pooled baseline rate, so the conversion below is illustrative:

| Baseline share with too little sleep (no device) | OR 2.17 (use) → approx. "times as likely" | OR 1.79 (access) → approx. "times as likely" |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 1.94× | 1.66× |
| 20% | 1.76× | 1.55× |
| 30% | 1.61× | 1.45× |
| 40% | 1.48× | 1.36× |
| 50% | 1.37× | 1.28× |

(Formula: RR = OR / (1 − p0 + p0 × OR).)

**Recommendation:**
- **Say:** "more than double the odds" / "more than twice the odds" for OR 2.17, and "about 1.8 times the odds" or "higher odds" for OR 1.79. "Linked to" or "associated with" is better than "causes".
- **Avoid:** "twice as likely", "2× more kids", "doubles the risk", "phones cut sleep in half". With a common outcome, the true "times as likely" is probably ~1.4–1.8×, not 2×.
- **If you want "more likely" language**, use it **without a multiplier** ("were more likely to get too little sleep", which is what the Carter abstract itself says), or use a study that reports a risk/prevalence ratio (Falbe 2015 PR 1.39; Nagata 2023 aRR 1.27). Those can be said as "39% / 27% more likely".
- **Hebrew note (for the native-speaker editor):** "סיכוי" in everyday Hebrew means probability, not odds. "פי 2 סיכוי" will be read as "twice as likely", which overstates the finding. Safer options: "יותר מכפול בסיכויים (odds)" is technically correct but clunky. Better is a version without a multiplier ("נמצאו בסבירות גבוהה יותר לישון פחות מדי") or the minutes-based backup fact (≈20 דקות פחות שינה). Have a native speaker confirm the final wording.

## 5. Caveats to keep in mind (and to answer critics)

1. **Correlation, not causation.** Every pooled estimate in Carter et al. comes from **cross-sectional** studies, and GRADE certainty is **low**. Children who keep phones in the bedroom may differ in other ways: they are older, have fewer family rules, sleep later for other reasons, or are anxious. Reverse causation is also possible, since kids who cannot sleep may reach for the phone. The prospective ABCD study (Nagata 2024) and the in-bed camera study (Brosnan 2024) strengthen the case for screens in the bedroom/in bed specifically, but they are still observational.
2. **Experiments are mixed.** Trials that limit screens show modest sleep gains: about +11 min/day in Martin 2021, +21 min in Bartel 2019 (teens, no control group). One good RCT in ~9-year-olds (Pedersen 2022) found **no** measurable sleep change after 2 weeks of reduced total screen use. Present the message as "a well-supported, sensible precaution backed by pediatricians", not "proven to add X hours of sleep".
3. **Age of samples.** Carter's sample averaged **14.5 years**, and most children were 10–18. Our audience's children are 5–11. The best age-matched data are Falbe 2015 (grades 4 and 7), ABCD (ages 9–14), Lund 2021 (6–12 subgroup), Tzischinsky & Haimov 2017 (Israel, age ~9) and Martin 2021 (trials, ages 2–13). On screen, "children" is acceptable, since Carter's inclusion was ages 6–19 and the authors use "children". Avoid implying the meta-analysis was done on 5–11-year-olds.
4. **"Access without use" is self-reported.** It means the device was in the sleep environment at least 3 times a week and the child reported not using it. It is still associational.
5. **Heterogeneity.** The headline OR 2.17 has I² = 90% (studies vary a lot), and its CI runs 1.42–3.32. "More than double" refers to the point estimate. The conservative statement is "higher odds".
6. **Date of evidence.** The Carter review covers studies published 2011–2015, before many young children had their own phones. Newer data (ABCD 2018–2021, Brosnan 2021) point the same way for in-bed and in-bedroom use.
7. **Do not use:** the daytime-sleepiness OR 2.72 as a headline (only 2 studies, wide CI), the melatonin "almost twice" figure as a phone claim (room light, tiny n), or the unverified Haifa 2014 press numbers.
